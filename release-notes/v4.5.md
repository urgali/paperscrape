# v4.5 — weather the size of the world it falls on

`versionCode = 36`, `versionName = "4.5"`. Baseline: v4.4 (`versionCode = 35`).

One subject: how big rain, snow and lightning are next to the people, cars and
buildings they fall around.

---

## What was wrong

v4.4 fixed rain disappearing on high-resolution screens by putting the effect on the
viewport scale. That was the right principle and the wrong amount: the sizes were
tripled on the reading that they had been tuned for the small test frame. They had
not been. Reported from a real phone, and then measured on rendered frames:

| | on a 1080×2424 frame | next to |
|---|---|---|
| raindrop | 1.52 m long | **1.15× the pedestrian beside it** |
| snowflake | 0.48 m across | **2× a head** |
| lightning bolt | 7.15 m tall | **taller than the tallest building in the scene** |

A raindrop as long as a person is not rain, and the number that said so was written
as a pixel count, which answers to nothing.

## What changed

**Rain, snow and lightning are now declared in metres**, like every other thing in the
scene — a house is 5.76 m, a car 1.45 m, an adult 1.75 m — and converted for the screen
by the one shared conversion the rest of the world already uses. A drop is 0.36–0.58 m,
a flake 0.13–0.30 m, a bolt 3.4–5.0 m.

**Rain is denser rather than bigger.** The real reason rain read as absent on a phone
was never that each drop was small: it was that there were only 90 of them spread over
the whole screen. Making each one nine times larger in area filled the gap with 90 big
sticks. There are now 240 slots, chosen by measuring: at 240 the curtain covers 86% of
the frame in a grid test against 46% before, with no dry gap wider than a third of the
screen, and the cost per frame is unchanged.

**Lightning is shorter.** A bolt reached 0.82 to 1.32 times the height of the tallest
painted building; it now reaches 0.53 to 0.78, and keeps its variety.

**The flash was measured and deliberately left alone.** The white wash a strike puts over
the frame covers all of it at 71% for a third of a second. It is strong, but a flash has
no size to be out of scale, and nothing measured shows it disproportionate — so it is a
decision to take on its own rather than something this release changed quietly.

**Falling leaves were measured and left alone too.** They are sized in absolute pixels
like precipitation used to be, but on a phone that lands them at 0.26 m, which reads
correctly. Nothing was changed on the strength of the technique alone.

## Six test images changed, and that is the point

The 360×800 frame the tests compare against had itself always drawn a raindrop as long
as a person. Six of the twenty-seven reference images changed — exactly the six that
contain rain or snow — and every other one is untouched, pixel for pixel.
